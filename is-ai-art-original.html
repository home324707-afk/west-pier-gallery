The debate · the law

Is AI art original?

The law has started to answer a narrower question: who, if anyone, owns it. It does not answer whether it is original in the sense an artist would mean. Checked on 2 November 2026.

United States

  • The Copyright Office’s January 2025 report concludes that copyright covers AI output only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements. Using AI to assist a person does not bar protection; purely AI-generated material is not protected.[1] [2]
  • On prompts, it says that with today’s generally available technology they “do not provide sufficient human control to make users of an AI system the authors of the output”, and that revising a prompt repeatedly does not change that.[1]
  • In Thaler v. Perlmutter the D.C. Circuit held that the Copyright Act requires a work to be authored in the first instance by a human being, so a machine cannot be the author.[3] The Supreme Court declined to hear the case on 2 March 2026, which leaves that ruling standing.[4]

United Kingdom

  • Since 1988, section 9(3) of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act has given a work “generated by computer in circumstances such that there is no human author” 50 years of protection, with the author being the person who made the arrangements for its creation. For a general-purpose AI that is usually the person who typed the prompt.[5]
  • The government noted a contradiction inside that provision: it covers only original works, and the modern test of originality is that a work is its author’s “own intellectual creation”, expressing creative choices and a “personal touch”.[5]
  • In its March 2026 report the government proposed removing this protection for wholly computer-generated works, while keeping copyright for works made with AI assistance. It also said it would keep monitoring, so nothing has changed in law yet.[6] [7]

What this does not settle

Copyright asks who authored a work. Originality in the artist’s sense asks something else: whether a work shows a new way of seeing. A court can decide that a machine cannot be an author without deciding that its pictures cannot move anyone. Both sides of that argument are on the wall.
