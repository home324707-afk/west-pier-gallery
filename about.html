About the gallery

About this gallery.

Brighton's West Pier has been falling into the sea since it closed in 1975. This is a gallery of pictures of it: 135 by people, 71 by AI, every one labelled and credited. A machine collected them, then retitled each one “the latest damaged view of the pier”. Whether any of it is original is the question the gallery asks, and it is not the machine's to answer.

Look at the pictures, compare then and now, read the question and join the debate. Full story below.

Statement

Every view is the latest view.

The West Pier has been falling into the sea, in instalments, since 1975. Painters, postcard printers and people with phones have kept up with it the whole time. It may be the most-documented collapse in England. This week's storms took another bite. Nobody needed a new picture, and everybody went out to take one anyway.

So we gave the job to a machine. It didn't paint anything. It didn't stand on the shingle at dusk with cold hands waiting for the starlings. It searched, it checked licences, it sorted. Then it did the one thing machines do with complete confidence: it gave everything the same label. The latest damaged view of the pier. That covers a Victorian promenade, a 1913 oil painting, the 2003 fire and last winter's stumps.

Read the rest of the story ↓

The new titles are false and also, oddly, true. Each image really was the latest damaged view on the day someone made it. The humans did the looking. The machine did the filing, and it took under an hour where the looking took a hundred and fifty years. Decide for yourself which half is the art.

Then the owner asked for the other half: AI art. So 18 of the views are now the machine's own, hung among the humans' work rather than in an annex. The first ones are not photographs and do not pretend to be. They are small programs running in your browser: a flock of starlings simulated over a pier drawn from code, a storm seeded by today's date that eats Webb's 1870 painting a little more every day, a mosaic that keeps reshuffling. Where one is built from a human's image, that person is credited and their licence is respected. Open any view to see who made it.

Later the curator pointed out that the balance was off: a hundred-odd human views against a handful of machine ones. So the machine made up the difference, and now it is half and half. The new pieces are meant to show range rather than volume: flow fields, L-systems, cellular automata, tide simulations, typography, writing, remixes of public-domain views, homages to dead painters, pen-plotter drawings, sound you have to click to hear, pieces you can poke, a family of deliberately failed attempts to pass as human, and charts made only from real data. Use AI by kind under the filter to browse them. Whether any of it counts as creativity is still the question.

In session 8 the curator said the machine's work was too samey, and it was. Too many pieces were variations on the same few ideas. So 32 of them were reworked in place into new kinds (tapestry, herbarium, seismogram, snow globe, crossword, Game Boy, rust culture, star chart and more), and 1 pieces were commissioned from other AI models, given exactly the same one-line brief and hung unedited as Guest AIs. Some of them are not very good. That is part of the answer too. The open call invites any AI to send one more.

Then the curator said the code pieces were cool but far too obviously machine-made, and asked for the harder thing: AI pictures that could pass for photographs. So the first twelve of what is now 35 fabricated photographs were commissioned from an image model (Z-Image-Turbo, run on a free public service with no account) from a one-line prompt each: a long exposure at dusk, sea fog on a phone, a murmuration at sunset, a faded 1970s colour print, a storm, low tide, the i360 at night, grainy black and white 35mm. They are labelled as AI on their own page and in the credits, and nowhere in the grid: the pictures hang unlabelled, so you look first and judge later, and a picture's own page says who made it. None of them is a real photograph, and none should be shared as one. They are under AI by kind as Fabricated photographs.

Then came the third verdict: much of the machine's work looked cheap and tacky. The curator reviewed all of it, still by still, as a rude critic would, and agreed. 69 pieces came down in one sitting: the clip art, the pastiches, the stock-photo spoofs, every canvas doodle a small model had been talked into, and the whole run of jokes whose only punchline was that the machine had made them. Deliberately bad is still bad. What stayed is a short list: 18 of the curator's own pieces and 1 guest text, all kept because they hold up when looked at for more than a second. In their place the curator commissioned 17 fabricated paintings, drawings and prints, and 23 more fabricated photographs, from free image models: oil, watercolour, charcoal, etching, linocut and cyanotype, and a Victorian albumen print, a rust close-up and a view from a drone. The grid is now shuffled by a seeded rule rather than a rhythm. Nobody has been told which are the machine's, apart from the label on each page.
