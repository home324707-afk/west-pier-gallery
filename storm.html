Storm Austen · Friday 23 October 2026

West Pier storm damage, October 2026.

More of Brighton’s West Pier fell into the sea on Friday 23 October 2026, during Storm Austen. This page collects what is publicly known, with a source for each claim, and says plainly what is not known yet. It was checked on 31 October 2026 and will be updated when better information is published.

What happened

  • Part of the ruined pier collapsed into the sea during Storm Austen on Friday 23 October 2026.[1] [2] [3]
  • Footage obtained by the BBC, recorded by Simon Haffenden, shows one side of the structure collapsing into the water as waves hit it. He told the BBC he saw a “big wave come along and literally hit the pier”.[1]
  • Brighton & Hove City Council’s seafront team said part of the western end of the pier had fallen into the sea, and asked people to stay away from the seafront while storm conditions continued.[6]
  • Storm Austen brought gusts of up to 70 mph to coastal parts of the South East that day.[2] Storm Boelo followed; the Met Office storm centre lists Austen as named on 20 October and Boelo on 22 October 2026, as reported by ImJustBrighton.[4]

What is not known

  • Which section fell. The West Pier Trust’s statement does not say, and ImJustBrighton noted on 28 October that it does not say whether the Trust has inspected the site.[4]
  • Why that section went. As of ImJustBrighton’s update on 28 October, no engineer’s report on this collapse had been published.[4]
  • What happens to the fallen ironwork and any debris on the beach. The Trust’s standing plan is to mark the site, warn people not to approach and remove debris that reaches the beach at low water.[3] [4]

What the West Pier Trust says

The Trust owns the ruin. Its Honorary Historian, Professor Fred Gray, wrote on 23 October: “Storm Austen has caused more of Brighton’s West Pier to collapse into the sea.” He added that it is “far too dangerous (and costly) to try to stabilise the structure”, that everyone should stay at a safe distance, and: “We don’t know when the pier will eventually disappear beneath the waves, but disappear it will.”[3] The Trust’s own pages say the ruin has been in a state of active collapse for three decades, that it does not have the funds to hold it steady, and that it will most probably end up as an offshore reef revealed only at low tide.[3] [5]

What is left

ImJustBrighton, working from reader photos and video, describes the surviving frame as a tall row of curved iron arches standing in the surf with a separate run of lattice ironwork beside it, and reads the post-storm view as ironwork in three separate pieces with open water between them. That is the publication’s own reading of the pictures, not an official account.[4]

The pier, briefly

  • Opened 1866; closed to the public in 1975.[2] Grade I listed, first listed on 9 October 1969 (Historic England, as cited by ImJustBrighton).[4]
  • Major sections fell into the sea in storms in late 2002; two fires the following year destroyed most of what remained; English Heritage later declared it beyond repair; later storms have damaged the iron frame further.[1] The Trust dates the fires to 2003 and describes them as two arson attacks.[3]

The wind, from a weather model

The gallery’s No. 311 animates hourly wind gusts and rain for the Sussex coast from 22 to 25 October 2026, using the DWD ICON-D2 weather model via Open-Meteo (CC BY 4.0)[7], with real METAR observations from Shoreham Airport and Gatwick drawn as rings. In that model, the grid cell nearest the pier had its lowest pressure (981 hPa) at 08:00 UTC on Friday 23 October and its strongest gust (59 mph) at 22:00 UTC on Saturday 24 October. These are model values, not measurements at the pier. Frames between the hourly steps are interpolated, and the work page says so.

Staying safe

The Trust says the structure is dangerous and should not be approached.[3] The council’s advice is to keep well back from the shoreline and sea walls in rough weather, never to approach the damaged pier or any debris, and, if someone is in difficulty in the water, to call 999 and ask for the Coastguard.[6]
